Why swarms, why now

The drone war outgrew the pilot.

Small drones now fly by the million. Each one still needs a trained pilot and an unbroken radio link, and both are running out.

Electronic warfare breaks the link between pilot and drone. In 2023, RUSI found Russian jamming systems spread roughly every ten kilometres along the front, and estimated Ukraine was losing about 10,000 drones a month1. Every new airframe also needs another pair of trained hands.

Builders are answering with autonomy. Ukrainian FPV drones now carry terminal-guidance modules that fly the last 400–500 metres on their own, built to push through jamming5. Defenders field interceptor drones that cost from about $1,500, against an estimated $30,000–50,000 for the Shahed they hunt6.

The next step is coordination: many drones, one operator, and no single link to cut. That is what we build. Homonoia writes the software that lets drones work as a swarm, and makes an airframe designed for it. A person sets the mission and makes every decision about force.

Technology

How the swarm thinks

A swarm is a distributed computer that flies. Ours works in four layers, from the rule each drone follows to the intent a person gives the group.

Local rules

Each drone keeps its distance, matches its neighbours’ heading and stays with the group: Reynolds’s three rules9. We limit each drone to its seven nearest neighbours, as starlings do2, so the swarm holds together as it stretches and compresses.

A mesh with no leader

Drones relay data to each other, so there is no lead aircraft to lose and no single link to jam. The US Perdix test showed it in 2016: 103 micro-drones released from fighter jets flew with no leader and adapted as drones joined or dropped out10.

Perception on board

Every drone sees and plans for itself. Ten small drones have threaded a bamboo forest on onboard cameras and computers alone11, and thirty have flocked outdoors with no central control12. We harden these methods against jamming, darkness and the loss of satellite navigation.

People set intent

An operator draws the area, sets the rules and approves actions. The swarm decides spacing, routes and who does what. Any use of force waits for a human decision.

Defence

Protect people and infrastructure

Counter-drone interception

Cheap interceptors against one-way attack drones. Interceptor drones cost about $1,500 to $16,000 depending on the model; the Shahed they hunt costs an estimated $30,000–50,000 to build6. A swarm covers more sky than a lone interceptor.

Reconnaissance under jamming

Sensing that keeps working when links fail, where single drones have been lost by the thousand1.

Layered air defence

Mobile sensor and interceptor layers for programmes such as the European Drone Defence Initiative7.

Civil

Find, map and reach people faster

Search and rescue

Speed from the air already saves minutes. In Sweden, drones delivered defibrillators before the ambulance in 67% of suspected cardiac arrests, a median 3 min 14 s sooner13. A swarm searches many places at once.

Demining survey

UN mine-action experts estimate 139,000 km² of Ukraine, over a fifth of its land, is contaminated by mines or unexploded ordnance14. Swarms map suspect ground from the air so deminers know where to start.

Disaster mapping

Many cameras build one live map of a flood, fire or earthquake zone, and crews work from it while the drones keep it current.

The law

Dual use is a legal category. EU Regulation 2021/821 covers items, including software and technology, that can be used for both civil and military purposes, and requires authorisation to export them15. We build for it: geofencing, end-use controls and full mission records in every system.

Doctrine

What we will build, and what we won’t.

Autonomy changes who decides. These five commitments are built into our software and our sales.

  1. I

    A person decides on force.

    Our systems search, track and intercept drones. Engaging any target needs a human decision. This follows the principle in US policy that weapons allow “appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force”16.

  2. II

    Limits are hard-coded.

    Every mission carries limits on area, time and behaviour. A drone that loses contact stays inside them, makes no new engagement and returns.

  3. III

    Everything is recorded.

    Commands, detections and machine decisions are logged and can be reviewed after every mission.

  4. IV

    Protection comes first.

    We build for interception, reconnaissance, and search and rescue.

  5. V

    We choose our customers.

    We sell to democratic governments, their armed forces and civil agencies, under export authorisation15.

Questions

Asked in every briefing

Is a swarm just a big light show?

No. A light show flies paths written in advance, from one computer: the record is 15,947 drones from a single machine4. If that computer or its link fails, the show is over. A swarm carries its coordination on board.

What happens when the link is jammed?

The drones keep their formation and keep flying the last approved task, inside its limits. They make no new engagement without a person, and they come home when the mission clock runs out. The lab above shows the idea.

Are swarms real yet?

Early, but real. Ukrainian units report using swarm software in combat more than 100 times, typically with three drones and sometimes eight, and testing groups of 253. Large swarms are still ahead of us, and that is the work.

Who decides to use force?

A person, every time. It is the first article of our doctrine and it follows the principle of “appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force” in US policy16.

Can civil agencies buy?

Yes. Search and rescue, demining survey and disaster mapping use the same autonomy with civil payloads and civil rules of operation.

Where do you sell?

To democratic governments, their armed forces and civil agencies, with export authorisation under EU dual-use rules where they apply15.
